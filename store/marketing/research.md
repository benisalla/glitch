# Glitch: what the research does and does not support

Prepared October 2026 for store copy. Every source below was opened and checked (title, authors, venue, year, DOI, and the finding quoted). Where we could only read the abstract, the table says so.

## Bottom line

1. **Supported:** a lot of phone use is habitual: short, repeated checks triggered by context (Oulasvirta et al. 2012). Habits run on stable cues, and changing those cues can disrupt them (Wood et al. 2005; Wood & Rünger 2016).
2. **Supported:** a fixed, predictable layout is what makes fast "tap by position" possible. When menu order changes, people get slower and most prefer a static order (Mitchell & Shneiderman 1989; Findlater & McGrenere 2004; Garaialde et al. 2020). A shuffled menu will reliably add that small search step.
3. **Supported, but for other designs:** small frictions such as a delay screen, grayscale, or moving apps off the home screen reduced app opens or screen time in field studies (Grüning et al. 2023; Haliburton et al. 2024; Holte & Ferraro 2020; Dekker & Baumgartner 2023; Kasturiratna et al. 2025). Effects are modest, often fade, and some studies were run by the tool's makers.
4. **Not supported:** no study we could find tests a shuffled or randomized app menu as a way to change phone habits. Nothing shows that Glitch itself reduces screen time or changes behavior.
5. **Not supported, and not allowed:** any claim about focus, memory, attention, intelligence, mental health, sleep, or "addiction". The owner's rules and Google Play's health-claims policy both rule these out.

**Direct evidence:** none. We searched for studies of randomized or shuffled app layouts, menus or home screens used to change habits or reduce use, and found none. The closest are studies of menus that change order, which only measured speed and preference (#5, #6), and one study that deliberately broke an interface habit (#4).

## Claims we CAN make

| Claim | Why it is OK |
|---|---|
| "Your apps appear in a new order every time you open the menu." | Describes the app. |
| "You can't tap by position, so you have to read the list to find an app." | Describes the app. Studies #5, #6 and #4 show that a changing order removes position-based speed. |
| "Breaks the habit of tapping by position / muscle memory for app locations." | Narrow and accurate: the shuffle prevents position-based tapping. Supported by #4, #5 and #6. Keep it to *tapping by position*, not phone habits in general. |
| "A small moment of friction before you open an app." | The shuffle adds a visual search step (#5, #6). Cox et al. (#9) describe this kind of microboundary. Don't say the friction *reduces* use. |
| "Inspired by research on design friction and habits." | True as a design rationale (#2, #7, #8, #9) if it stays that vague. Don't say "proven by" or "backed by studies". |
| "No permissions, no internet, no tracking. Your app list never leaves your phone." | Describes the app (see AGENTS.md and the manifest). |
| "Opens apps normally. No timers, blockers or nagging." | Describes the app. |

## Claims we must NOT make

| Claim | Why not |
|---|---|
| "Improves focus / attention / memory / makes you smarter / trains your brain." | Banned by the owner's AGENTS.md. No study tests this for a shuffled menu. Google Play forbids unverified health claims. |
| "Reduces screen time" / "cuts phone use by X%" / "use your phone less". | Never tested for this app. The one sec numbers (#7, #8) come from a different mechanism (wait screen plus cancel button) whose strongest effect came from the cancel option, which we lack. Borrowing them would be misleading. |
| "Clinically proven", "science-backed", "research shows Glitch works". | There is no direct study (see "Direct evidence" above). |
| "Cures / fights phone addiction", "reduces anxiety / depression", "better sleep", "digital detox". | Health or medical claims. Even the grayscale studies (#10, #11) found no specific effect on anxiety, depression or sleep. |
| "Makes you more mindful / intentional." | Promises a mental-state outcome. #8 suggests more intentional opening only for one sec, with self-selected users and no baseline. |
| "Breaks your phone addiction / bad habits" (general). | Too broad. #11 found checking habits survived a UI change ("unlocks did not change"). #8 and #16 found effects fade or rebound. |
| "Replaces your launcher / changes your Samsung app drawer." | Not true (AGENTS.md platform boundary). |
| Quoting specific study numbers next to our app name (e.g. "57% fewer opens"). | Implies the number applies to us. Cite studies only as background, if at all. |

## Honest caveats to keep in mind

- Changing the order makes finding apps slower, and most people in menu studies preferred a fixed order (#5). That *is* the product, but expect some users to find it annoying. #13 found that a friction bundle raised stress.
- Friction effects in the literature are modest, decline over time, and often come back when the tool is removed (#8, #16, #20).
- Two of the strongest friction studies (#7, #8) include the developer of the app they test as a co-author.
